About the course

An advanced undergraduate and graduate seminar on NeuroAI, the joint study of artificial and biological intelligence. We look at what modern foundation models learn and how they are trained, how far interpretability methods let us see inside them, and how well their representations and behavior line up with data from brains and people.

The influence runs in both directions. AI methods suggest new mechanistic hypotheses and experiments for neuroscience and cognitive psychology, and neuroscience and cognitive science suggest architectures, training curricula and evaluations that address the limitations of current AI.

Each week opened with a lecture on Tuesday. On Thursday, small groups of students gave five-minute lightning talks on the papers listed under each week below, followed by a discussion. The semester closed with a poster session on student projects and a series of guest lectures.

This page archives the Spring 2026 offering: background readings, the papers students presented, and my lecture slides.

Students come to this course from neuroscience, psychology, computer science and engineering. In week 2 everyone worked through the same short core, then picked the refreshers that matched the gaps in their background.

Project

Students ran behavioral experiments on language and vision–language models, treating the models as participants in the way cognitive psychologists treat people. Most projects started from Psych-101, the trial-by-trial data from 160 psychology experiments used to train Centaur (Binz et al., 2025). Each pair compared at least two models with each other and with human data, asked whether the models showed human-like behavioral signatures, and controlled for the confounds that make such comparisons misleading. They presented the results at a poster session.
